# Is the U.S. a Lucky Survivor? A Hierarchical Bayesian Approach

This paper quantifies the extent of survivorship bias in the U.S. stock market. In this paper, they construct a comprehensive database with total return indices from a cross-section of 55 countries in the past century (1920–2020).

In this context, the survivorship bias suggests that we misprice U.S. assets because we treat a random outlier (the U.S.'s great stock performance) as a reproducible blueprint.

The model they use models how a rational investor would learn about crash probabilities by observing the entire world.

The investor tries to figure out:  

1. How likely is a U.S. market crash?  
2. How much of the crash risk is U.S.-specific vs. global?  
3. How should beliefs update when other countries crash or do not crash?

If you look back to 1900, the U.S. was just one of many "emerging markets" along with Germany, Russia, and Argentina.

Investors see that because in the last 100 years many emerging economies have had large stock market wipeouts. Compared to the U.S., they assume the probability of failure for the U.S. is very low.

For example, if you had looked at Argentina in the data in 1900, a Bayesian model would have heavily bet on them.

While the rest of the world was crashing, the U.S. kept winning, showing that the probability an investor assigns to a U.S. collapse began to diverge from the global average.

The paper identified the difference between the realized returns (what actually happened) and the expected return. That luck, or the fact that the U.S. simply avoided crashes that were statistically likely to happen based on global data, played a role, while also reflecting that investors learned the U.S. was safer than they originally thought.

Fundamentally, when looking at the data from 2000 onwards, there are no more global crashes. Because global crashes have become rarer and the U.S. avoided the earlier big crashes, the model (and investors) think the U.S. is exceptionally safe.

The model builds in the fact that each country has differences in institutional quality, volatility, growth dynamics, idiosyncratic crash risk, and other country-specific parameters. This allows the model to answer: "Even with the U.S. having better institutional qualities and innovation capacity, is it still an outlier?" The answer is yes.

The market prices U.S. stocks as if continued dominance is a structural certainty rather than a statistical probability, repeatedly updated by winning a series of historical coin flips. Investors treat the U.S. as a "safe haven" because it has never failed.

The paper concluded that about one-third of the return that the U.S. had garnered was not a reward for risk but a reward for being an outlier, or a "survival bonus."

The U.S. did not have better "DNA"; it just avoided catastrophes.

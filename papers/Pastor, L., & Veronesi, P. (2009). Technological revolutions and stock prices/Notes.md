# Technological Revolutions and Stock Prices

This paper shows how new technologies can produce price spikes and crashes that look like bubbles, even when investors act perfectly rationally.

Whenever a big new technology appears, investors usually get excited about it, causing bubble-like behavior that is often written off as market irrationality.

However, even if all market participants are rational, bubble-like behavior can still happen.

When new technologies appear, they naturally create uncertainty. Questions like:

- How large will the market be?
- How profitable will the market be?
- Which firms will survive (as most will end up failing)?
- How fast will adoption happen?

Early investors in these companies have a huge potential upside. However, they usually end up overpaying because the probability of this upside is uncertain.

As rational investors price in the possibility of huge future profits, this causes prices early on to be high, creating an initial boom.

As information arrives, beliefs are updated rationally, such as the technical realities of the industry, and this can cause the stock to move wildly.

Because the upside might be enormous, the stock of an innovative firm behaves like a real option — a bet on a huge but uncertain future payoff. The option premium comes from uncertainty plus an asymmetric payoff. When the uncertainty disappears, the collapse of the option value causes the price to fall.

The distribution of returns is extremely skewed, with most firms failing and a few giving extraordinary returns.

Even if the expected value is high, the median return for the median investor is poor.

Historical booms — the 1800s railroads, 1900s electricity, 1920s mass production, 1960s electronics, 1980s biotech, and the 1990s internet — have all followed the cycle.

**Big excitement → high prices → crash → survivors become giants.**

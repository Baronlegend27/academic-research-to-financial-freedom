# The Equity Premium

The purpose of the Fama and French paper is to examine the gap between the risk‑free rate and expected stock returns. They estimate expected capital gains using dividend growth and earnings growth. These measures imply expected equity premiums of **2.55%** (dividend growth) and **4.32%** (earnings growth), far below the **7.43%** realized premium from 1951–2000.

Their central argument is that the high realized stock returns of the second half of the 20th century were **not** the result of high expected returns. Instead, they were driven by a **large, unexpected decline in discount rates**.

The discount rate is the return investors demand to hold stocks. When discount rates fall, investors are willing to pay more for the same future cash flows. Prices rise immediately, but expected returns going forward fall because investors are accepting a lower required return.

A simple example illustrates this. Imagine a rental property that pays $10,000 per year. If investors demand a 10% return, the property is worth $100,000. If investors demand only 5%, the same property is worth $200,000. The owner earns a large capital gain, even though nothing changed about the rent.

This mechanism applies directly to the U.S. stock market from the 1950s to the 1990s. Interest rates fell, inflation fell, and risk premia fell. As a result, discount rates declined and valuations rose sharply.

The U.S. benefited more than other countries because its exceptional historical stability — avoiding wars, hyperinflation, and political breakdown — allowed discount rates to fall steadily over the 20th century. But discount rates cannot fall indefinitely, and the U.S. is already priced as if they will. This makes the U.S. more vulnerable than cheaper global markets if discount rates stay high or fall only modestly.

If discount rates fall globally in 2026, cheaper markets will experience larger valuation boosts, while the U.S. will experience a smaller one because it is already expensive.

The paper emphasizes that the equity premium is fundamentally an **expected** value. Using recent realized returns to estimate future expected returns is misleading when discount rates change over time. The earlier period (1872–1950) had lower realized returns that were more consistent with the expected returns implied by dividend and earnings growth.

Overall, expected returns are likely closer to the **2.5–4.5%** range implied by fundamentals, not the **7%** historical average.

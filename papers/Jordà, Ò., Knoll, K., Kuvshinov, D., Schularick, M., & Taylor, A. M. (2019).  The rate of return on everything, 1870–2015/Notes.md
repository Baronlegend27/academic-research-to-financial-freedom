
# Real Equity Returns in *“The Rate of Return on Everything, 1870–2015”*

The paper finds that **global real equity returns averaged about 6–7% per year** from 1870–2015. This number is higher than many people expect, and it reflects the *historical environment*, not necessarily the future.

---

## Why Historical Real Returns Were So High

### **1. Dividend yields were much higher historically**
- Early and mid‑20th century dividend yields were often **3–5%**.  
- Today they are closer to **1–2%**.  
High dividends alone pushed historical returns upward.

### **2. Strong long‑run earnings growth**
- Industrialization, globalization, and rapid productivity growth boosted real earnings.  
- This added another **1–2%** to real returns.

### **3. Valuation expansion over the century**
- Price‑to‑earnings ratios rose significantly, especially in the U.S.  
- Rising valuations artificially increased historical returns.  
- This is a **one‑time tailwind**, not a permanent feature.

### **4. Survivorship and U.S. exceptionalism**
- The U.S. avoided war destruction and benefited from global dominance.  
- U.S. markets outperformed most countries, pulling up the global average.

---

## Why These Returns Do *Not* Predict the Future

### **1. Dividend yields are much lower today**
Lower yields mathematically reduce future expected returns.

### **2. Valuations are already high**
- The past century saw valuations rise.  
- They cannot rise indefinitely.  
- If valuations stay flat or fall, future returns will be lower.

### **3. Growth is slower**
- Population growth, productivity growth, and globalization are all slowing.  
- Slower earnings growth → lower long‑run returns.

### **4. The U.S. was an outlier**
- The paper explicitly warns that the U.S. is **not representative**.  
- Global returns were lower, and future U.S. returns may converge toward global norms.

---

## What This Implies for Expected Returns

The paper does **not** give a forecast, but its data strongly implies:

- **Historical U.S. real return:** ~6.5–7%  
- **Minus valuation expansion tailwind:** ~1–2%  
- **Minus lower dividends and slower growth:** ~1%  

**→ Plausible long‑run expected real return: ~4–5%**

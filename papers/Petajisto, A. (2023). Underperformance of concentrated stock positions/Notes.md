# Underperformance of concentrated stock positions

The paper states that many wealthy investors often hold large positions in one stock (10–20%) of their portfolio.

Often this includes founders holding company shares, executives paid in stock, and investors who bought a big winner.

The paper wants to answer the question: is holding a big position in one stock actually a good idea long-term?

The paper uses all U.S. stocks from 1926 to 2022. It looks at long-term performance, focusing on 10-year holding periods for individual stocks compared to the market.

For each stock, the 10-year cumulative return is compared to the 10-year cumulative return of the overall market.

This is measured as the **relative return**:

$$
\text{Relative Return} = \text{Stock 10-year Return} - \text{Market 10-year Return}
$$

It tells us how each stock performs relative to a broad market index.

They find the median stock (the stock in the middle of the distribution of relative returns) has a median relative return of -7.9% over 10 years, which is about -0.82% per year.

The paper looks at “recent winners”, which are stocks that did really well in the past 1–5 years. Specifically, they define these as the top 20% of stocks based on their returns over the previous 5 years.

They find that even recent winners usually underperform the market over the next 10 years, with a 55–60% chance of doing worse than the overall market.

They also find that stocks which have underperformed the market in the last 1–5 years tend to do better than average, with a similar 55–60% probability of outperforming the market over the next 10 years.

This pattern reflects the commonly observed effect of mean reversion in stock returns. Stocks that performed exceptionally well or poorly in the past tend to move back toward average performance over time. In other words, recent winners are more likely to underperform, while recent losers have a higher chance of outperforming, as extreme past returns often “normalize” in the long run.

The paper uses the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index as a measure of concentration in a market portfolio.

## Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) Using Percentages

$$
\text{HHI} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} (p_i)^2
$$

Where:  
- $p_i$ = share of firm/asset $i$ in **percent** (e.g., 25 for 25%)  
- $N$ = total number of firms/assets

**Note:**  
- The HHI ranges from 0 to 10,000, with 0 indicating maximum diversification and 10,000 indicating complete concentration.
- Example: Two assets each with 50% share → HHI = 50² + 50² = 5000

The paper defines holding a stock as about 10% of a portfolio being meaningfully concentrated. 

## Real Example

Emory University in 1979 received a donation of Coca-Cola stock from a wealthy donor.

In the following years, the stock value exploded, and by 2000, half of Emory's $5 billion endowment was in Coca-Cola.

This was significant because Coca-Cola’s highs were in 1998, and from that time to 2000, the total value of the Coca-Cola stock had lost 30%, or $1 billion from the position’s peak, while the broader market had risen 30% over the same period.
The staff at Emory justified holding the Coca-Cola position by saying, "It may have recently underperformed, but historically the returns were good."

The COO argued in 2000 that they were still better off than if they had owned a diversified equity portfolio over the previous 15 years.

The stock recovered a little, but over the next five years, it still fell 25% compared to the market.

Eventually, Emory reduced Coca-Cola to about 10% of their endowment.

Emory’s big investment in Coca-Cola did well until mid-1997, but after that, the university would have done much better with a diversified stock portfolio for the next 25 years.

## Conculsions of the paper

Investors often hold on to their biggest winners for too long. Even if these stocks later recover after losing value, it is usually very hard for them to catch up to the overall market.

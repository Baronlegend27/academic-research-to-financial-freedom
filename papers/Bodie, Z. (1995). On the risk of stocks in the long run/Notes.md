# Zvi Bodie’s (1995) “On the Risk of Stocks in the Long Run”

It is often said that stocks become less risky over time because stock prices fluctuate frequently in the short run, whether daily, weekly, monthly, or even yearly. However, over longer periods, for example 20 years, the average return tends to stabilize due to the law of large numbers.

This gives rise to the idea that:

> “The longer you hold stocks, the lower the risk of earning a negative average return.”

In his 1995 paper, Bodie argues that this widely held belief that stocks become safer over long horizons is incorrect.

He defines risk in terms of the cost of insuring a stock portfolio against earning less than the risk free rate, such as Treasury bills.

Using a simplified Black Scholes framework, he shows that the cost of this insurance, for example by buying a put option, increases with the investment horizon.

According to this model, the longer you hold stocks, the more expensive it becomes to insure against underperformance relative to the risk free return.

The reason this challenges the common belief is that even if the expected average return is stable, the distribution of possible outcomes becomes wider over time. As a result, the chance that the portfolio significantly underperforms the risk free asset can still be substantial.

Even if the average direction or long term trend is positive, the maximum possible deviation from that trend increases with time. Insurance must compensate for that increasing potential downside risk.

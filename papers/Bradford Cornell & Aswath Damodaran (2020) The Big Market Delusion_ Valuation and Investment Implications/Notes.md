# The Big Market Delusion: Valuation and Investment Implications

The idea of a “big market” often leads entrepreneurs, venture capitalists, and public investors to overestimate the future growth and profitability of companies targeting that market. While massive markets are enticing, the promise of scale can distort expectations and drive valuations to unsustainable levels.  

Entrepreneurs and investors are frequently captivated by enormous markets such as ride sharing, online advertising, or cannabis because they envision vast numbers of customers, revenue, and profits. This optimism can push company valuations extremely high even before the business has proven its viability.

---

## The Three Ingredients of the Big Market Delusion

1. **Market Size Attracts Players:** A large or perceived large market naturally draws businesses seeking opportunity.  
2. **Overconfidence Bias:** Entrepreneurs and venture capitalists often overestimate their chances of success and the potential profits they can capture.  
3. **Pricing Dynamics:** Investors frequently follow what others are paying, creating a disconnect between valuations and underlying fundamentals.  

Comapines often pitch enormus total addressabel markets (TAMs) but these usually ignore competition and assume unrealsitic adoption rates.

While targeting a big market can enable rapid scaling and economies of scale, success depends on more than size alone. Capturing significant market share, generating sustainable profits, and defending against competitors are critical for realizing value.  

---

## Overconfidence and Collective Bias

Entrepreneurs and venture capitalists tend to be overly confident about their products and investment choices. When multiple players enter the same large market, this overconfidence can amplify collective overpricing. Studies consistently show that many entrepreneurs and VCs overestimate their likelihood of success, creating a systemic bias across industries.  

Instead of just one company being overprices, all compaines in the sectors can be overpriced. As investors assume mulitple winners. 

Value is ultimately based on expected cash flows and growth, whereas price is influenced by supply, demand, and market sentiment. Early-stage markets often become “pricing games,” where momentum and investor mood drive valuations more than fundamentals. This is especially true in big, uncertain, or winner-take-all markets.  

---

## Historical Examples

- **Dot-com E-commerce (1990s):** Massive valuations were fueled by anticipated revenue growth; many companies collapsed when the bubble burst.  
- **Online Advertising (2015–2019):** Rapid market growth attracted new entrants, with valuations often justified by aggressive future revenue projections.  
- **Cannabis Market (2019):** Early-stage enthusiasm drove high valuations despite uncertain fundamentals.  

By 2015, the total online advertising market was estimated at $466 billion. Yet, the imputed revenues of publicly traded companies already exceeded this, signaling overpricing relative to actual market size. By 2019, market maturation led to fewer surviving companies, more aligned market caps, and increased investor demand for clear paths to profitability, impacting companies like Snapchat and Twitter.  

The cannabis market experienced a similar cycle. Legalization in Canada and select U.S. states created rapid growth, with global revenue expectations rising from roughly $50 billion in 2018 to $150 billion by 2024. By October 2019, overconfidence reversed: Tilray dropped 80.7% and Canopy Growth 38.6%—despite no significant change in fundamentals.  

### Lesser-Known Example

During the Vietnam War, there was a large demand for nickel due to its usage in military hardware.
During 1969, the world's largest nickel producer, Inco, which was a Canadian mining company, had a major labor strike, causing the price of nickel to go up.
In September 1969, Poseidon NL, a small Australian exploration company, announced it had discovered nickel.
Before the discovery, Poseidon's shares were about $0.80; after the announcement, investors rushed to buy the stock. Then, after drilling results were released, the price rose to $12.30, as people believed it might be one of the largest nickel discoveries ever.

Within months, the share price went to about $280, which was an increase of about 35,000% in a period of months.

This hype spread across the entire Australian mining sector, affecting any company that had mentioned nickel, with many new mining companies listed on the stock exchange just to attract money.
In early 1970, the bubble burst, as nickel prices started to fall, the deposits were not as rich as first believed, and actual mining and transportation costs were much higher than expected.

Even though there was actually nickel in the ground, the company struggled because the mine was expensive to build, nickel prices dropped, and financing became difficult. By 1976, Poseidon was unable to pay its debts, and shareholders lost basically all their investment. 

Anyone who chased the Poseidon bubble or the broader nickel mining hype ended up losing money, leaving no lasting wealth for investors or the industry. Even though Poseidon itself acted legitimately and was run properly, unavoidable business realities such as mining costs, infrastructure, and transportation challenges meant the company could not deliver the returns investors expected

## Common Features of Big Market Delusions

- **Big Market Stories:** High valuations are justified by market size, often without explaining how the company will capture it.  
- **Blindness to Competition:** Growth potential is assumed without accounting for existing or new competitors.  
- **Focus on Growth:** Companies prioritize rapid expansion over sustainable business models or profitability.  
- **Disconnect from Fundamentals:** Stock prices frequently detach from earnings, assets, or realistic revenue potential.

Big markets encourage growth-at-all-costs strategies, weak unit economics, and delayed or non-existent portability.

---

## Market Dynamics and Corrections

Price run-ups are influenced by capital availability; markets with abundant funding, like the U.S., often experience faster spikes. Corrections vary: the dot-com bubble burst quickly, online advertising moderated gradually, and cannabis rose and fell sharply. Accurately predicting the timing of corrections remains extremely difficult.  

---

## Investment Implications

- **Momentum Investors:** Enthusiasm for big markets can drive rapid stock price growth, but corrections are unpredictable.  
- **Value Investors:** Avoiding growth stocks can protect against overpricing, though it may risk missing long-term gains.  
- **Valuation Professionals:** Peer-based multiples can obscure collective overpricing; discounted cash flow models with conservative assumptions are more reliable for detecting mispricing.

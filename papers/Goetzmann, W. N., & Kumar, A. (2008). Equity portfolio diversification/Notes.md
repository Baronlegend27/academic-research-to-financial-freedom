# Summary of Goetzmann & Kumar (2008): Equity Portfolio Diversification

This paper analyzes more than **40,000 brokerage accounts** over a six‑year period, examining the actual stocks investors held and the risks those portfolios created. The goal is to understand **real‑world diversification behavior** rather than theoretical portfolio construction.

The authors find that **most investors hold too few stocks**, resulting in portfolios with **significant idiosyncratic (avoidable) risk**. This under‑diversification exposes investors to unnecessary volatility that could be reduced through broader asset holdings.

### Measurement of Diversification The study quantifies diversification using: 
- **Number of stocks held** in each portfolio.
- **Idiosyncratic risk**, calculated as the portion of total portfolio variance not explained by market factors.
- **Exposure to high‑volatility and high‑skewness stocks**, which signals preference for lottery‑like payoffs.
- **Concentration metrics**, such as the share of portfolio value in the top few holdings.


### Statistical and Econometric Techniques The authors apply: 
- **Cross‑sectional regressions** linking diversification levels to investor demographics (age, income, education, trading experience).
- **Behavioral‑trait proxies**, such as turnover (for overconfidence), local stock holdings (for familiarity bias), and trend‑following indicators.
- **Risk decomposition** using historical stock return data to separate systematic and idiosyncratic components.
- **Comparative analysis** across investor subgroups to identify patterns in under‑diversification.
 

## Psychological and Practical Drivers of Under‑Diversification

### Concentration Driven by Investor Psychology
The paper shows that investors do not simply forget to diversify; they are often guided by psychological tendencies that push them toward concentrated positions. Many become overconfident and believe they have superior insight into a particular stock, leading them to invest more heavily in it than is rational. Investors also gravitate toward stocks that capture their attention, such as companies in the news or familiar local firms, rather than conducting a broad search for the best overall fit for their portfolio. Some approach the market with a lottery mindset, choosing a few highly volatile stocks in the hope of a large payoff even though the probabilities suggest they are more likely to lose money.

### Naive Diversification and Misunderstood Risk
Although many investors understand the idea of diversification, they still build portfolios that ignore how assets move together. Instead of constructing balanced portfolios, they fall into patterns shaped by psychology, familiarity, and convenience. A common example is naive diversification, where someone buys several different stocks that behave similarly, often because they come from the same industry, creating the illusion of safety without the substance.

### Skewness Preference and Overconfidence
A major driver of concentrated portfolios is skewness preference. Some investors are not aiming for steady, compounding wealth; they are chasing the small chance of a life‑changing payoff. This lottery‑like mindset pushes them toward concentrated bets rather than broad diversification. Overconfidence reinforces this tendency, as investors convince themselves they have special insight into a particular company or sector even when the evidence suggests otherwise.

### Familiarity Biases
Familiarity plays a significant role in shaping investment choices. Local bias leads people to buy companies headquartered nearby simply because they feel more comfortable with what they recognize. Professional bias leads individuals to invest heavily in the industry they work in, such as tech workers buying tech stocks or healthcare workers buying healthcare stocks, which ends up doubling their exposure to the same economic risks that could affect both their employment and their portfolio.

### Search Costs and Attention‑Driven Investing
Practical constraints also influence investor behavior. The market is enormous, and search costs are real. Researching hundreds of companies is time‑consuming, so investors gravitate toward what is already capturing their attention. They buy attention‑grabbing stocks, the ones trending on social media, highlighted in financial news, or showing dramatic price swings. Even when they attempt to diversify, they often do so superficially, spreading money across several well‑known names that all move in lockstep.

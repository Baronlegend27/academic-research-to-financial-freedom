# Do stocks outperform Treasury bills? & Do global stocks outperform US Treasury bills?

The full-sample buy-and-hold return is the return you would earn by purchasing the asset at the start of the time series. You simply hold the asset and reinvest any dividends throughout the period.

In these papers wealth creation is the equation:

$$
\text{Wealth Creation} = \text{Final Market Value} - \text{Initial Market Value} \times (1 + R_{\text{T-bill}})^T
$$

Where: 
- $R_{\text{T-bill}}$ = compounded return of rolling 1-month Treasury bills  
- $T$ = number of months the stock existed

This is important because it gives a dollar amount not a percentage. As often, two stocks have the same percentage return but much different wealth creation.

A tiny biotech going from $5M to $20M creates $15M in wealth.

While Apple going from $500B to $2.5T creates $2 trillion in wealth. 

Even if a stock xyz started with market cap $1B and ends with $1.5B.
While during the time T-bills would have turned $1B into $1.7B over the same period. Then -$200M in wealth would have been destroyed. 

Once you compute wealth creation for all the stocks, the distribution is extremly right-skewed. As most stocks create little or negative wealth.

A majority of global stocks, about 56 percent of U.S. stocks and 61 percent of non‑U.S. stocks, underperformed one month U.S. Treasury bills when looking at full sample buy and hold returns.

Average stock returns are still positive: Even though most individual stocks underperform cash, average returns across all stocks were positive over long horizons (e.g., annualized and decade horizons), and the aggregate global stock market did outperform T‑bills when viewed in total.

Around 1.3% of global stocks accounted for essentially all global stock market wealth creation relative to T‑bills.

The (2020) study examines nearly 62,000 global common stocks from January 1990 to December 2018, including both U.S. and non‑U.S. firms.

The paper assigns a −30% return to stocks that delisted without a final return to avoid survivorship bias, which occurs when only surviving stocks are analyzed. This adjustment ensures that poor-performing or failed firms are included, giving a realistic picture of stock performance. Without it, the study would overestimate returns and obscure the finding that most stocks underperform Treasury bills, while only a few generate the majority of wealth.
# Results
A majority of individual stocks, about 56 percent of U.S. and 61 percent of non-U.S. stocks, underperform one month U.S. Treasury bills on a full sample compound return basis, meaning most stocks grow less than a safe cash investment over their lifetime. However, when looking at wealth creation, the actual dollar value added for investors, almost all gains come from a tiny fraction of companies. The top approximately 1.3 percent of global firms account for essentially all 44.7 trillion dollars in wealth created above T-bills from 1990 to 2018, showing that a few huge winners drive the market's overall success.

Put simply, half of all stocks make more than super-safe government bonds, but almost all the extra money the stock market earns comes from just a tiny handful of superstar companies. Most stocks barely move the needle

A few extremely successful stocks generate massive wealth while most stocks create little or negative wealth, which explains why aggregate market indices outperform safe assets even though most individual stocks fail to do so.

The broad diversification or index investing is necessary to capture the rare big winners.
